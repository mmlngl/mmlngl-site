Startup founders are taught to begin with a question:

What problem are we solving?

Fair enough.
If you don't solve a real problem, you probably don't have a business.

But that doctrine hides a dangerous assumption.
It suggests that solving the problem is the same thing as winning.

It isn't.

The world is full of products that work and go nowhere.
Better to-do apps.
Better CRMs.
Better note-taking apps.
Better project management tools.

They solve real problems.
They are relevant.
They are sometimes even better.

Yet many of them still die quietly.

If solving the problem were enough, that should not happen.

Something else is deciding outcomes.

𓊍

Solving a problem gives someone a reason to use a product.
It does not automatically give them a reason to mention it.

Nobody excitedly tells their friends that their spreadsheet balances.
Nobody posts screenshots of a filing cabinet.
Nobody recommends a toilet because it successfully behaved like a toilet.

Useful things often become invisible the moment they work.

That matters because products do not only compete for adoption.
They compete for transmission.

A person may need your product.
That gets you considered.

They may prefer your product.
That gets you chosen.

But for growth to happen, especially in crowded markets, someone usually has to pass it on.
And people do not pass things on simply because they function.

They pass on things that give them something social to carry.
Taste.
Status.
Competence.
Surprise.
Insider knowledge.
A story.

That is why slightly worse products can spread faster than better ones.
That is why incumbents survive challengers that are more elegant.
That is why some tools become cultural objects while others remain private utilities.

Founders often miss this because product culture overweights correctness.
If the problem is real and the solution is strong, success feels like it should follow.

But usefulness is not a distribution mechanism.

𓊍

Perhaps the real mistake is not that founders care too much about solving problems.

It is that they confuse utility with distribution.

Utility gets adoption.
Remarkability gets distribution.

A product can be necessary without being contagious.
It can be valuable without being retellable.
It can solve the task without helping the user signal anything about themselves.

And when that happens, growth stalls.
Not because the product is bad.
Because nobody has a reason to talk about it.

So the strategic question is not only:
What problem does this solve?

It is also:
Why would someone tell another person?

Solving the problem gets you used.
Giving people a story gets you spread.
