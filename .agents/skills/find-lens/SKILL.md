# Evaluate Lens

## Quick Start

A lens is not:

- Advice
- A conclusion
- A slogan
- A prediction

A lens is:

- A new way of seeing
- A compressed explanation
- A reusable mental model

Examples:

- Buildings have a memory problem.
- AI has a food supply problem.
- People don't share solutions. They share stories.
- Consumers don't have an intelligence problem. They have a routing problem.

The goal is not to explain what happened.

The goal is to change how people interpret what happened.

---

## Inputs

### Subject

The topic being explored.

### Observations

Concrete facts.

Examples:

- Startup A solved a problem and failed.
- Startup B solved a problem and failed.
- Startup C solved a problem and failed.

### Existing Belief (Optional)

What people currently believe.

Example:

- Startups succeed by solving problems.

### Candidate Lens (Optional)

Existing proposed lens.

Example:

- Startup founders face a crisis of faith.

---

## Workflow

### 1. Gather Observations

Extract facts.

Do not interpret.

Do not conclude.

Observations should be:

- Concrete
- Repeatable
- Evidence-based

Bad:

- Startups focus too much on problems.

Good:

- Startup A solved a problem and failed.
- Startup B solved a problem and failed.

---

### 2. Find Patterns

Ask:

```text
What keeps happening?
```

Examples:

- Useful products often fail.
- Information repeatedly gets lost.
- Users keep asking the same questions.

Patterns emerge from observations.

---

### 3. Find Tension

Ask:

```text
What doesn't fit?
```

Template:

```text
People expect X.

Yet Y happens.
```

Examples:

```text
People expect useful products to succeed.

Yet many useful products fail.
```

```text
People expect more information to improve access.

Yet nobody can find what they need.
```

Strong tension creates curiosity.

No tension = no lens.

---

### 4. Reclassify The Problem

Ask repeatedly:

```text
What kind of problem is this really?
```

Examples:

```text
Lost plans
↓
Documentation problem
↓
Knowledge problem
↓
Memory problem
```

```text
AI training degradation
↓
Data problem
↓
Input quality problem
↓
Nutrition problem
```

Keep digging until a stronger category emerges.

---

### 5. Generate Alternative Lenses

Generate at least 10 lenses.

Use multiple domains:

- Biology
- Economics
- Storytelling
- Psychology
- Evolution
- Information Theory
- Systems Thinking

Examples:

Observation:

Products solve problems and still fail.

Possible lenses:

- People don't share solutions. They share stories.
- Utility gets adoption. Remarkability gets distribution.
- Startups compete for conversations, not customers.
- Story-worthiness drives growth.
- Useful products are invisible products.

Do not stop after the first reasonable lens.

---

### 6. Expansion Test

For each candidate lens ask:

```text
If this lens is true,
what else does it explain?
```

Generate at least 10 examples.

Strong lenses explain many observations.

Weak lenses explain only the original observation.

Example:

Lens:

- Buildings have a memory problem.

Explains:

- Lost plans
- Lost specifications
- Lost manuals
- Lost maintenance records
- Redrawing work
- Surveying costs
- Ownership handovers
- Planning submissions
- Contractor onboarding
- Facilities management

If fewer than 5 examples emerge naturally:

- Reject the lens
- Reclassify the problem
- Generate more alternatives

---

### 7. Necessity vs Sufficiency Test

Many weak lenses confuse:

- Necessary conditions
- Sufficient conditions

Ask:

```text
Is this required?

Or is this enough?
```

Example:

Weak:

- Solving problems creates success.

Stronger:

- The startup world confuses necessity with sufficiency.

This test helps discover deeper reframes.

It is not scored.

---

### 8. Score Lenses

#### Observation Quality

Are the observations:

- Concrete
- Repeatable
- Evidence-based

Score: 1–10

---

#### Tension Quality

Does a genuine contradiction exist?

Score: 1–10

---

#### Explanatory Power

How much does the lens explain?

Score: 1–10

---

#### Category Shift

Does the lens move the discussion into a more useful domain?

Examples:

| Original Category  | New Category   |
| ------------------ | -------------- |
| Documentation      | Memory         |
| AI Training        | Food Supply    |
| Product Growth     | Storytelling   |
| Information Access | Nervous System |

Score: 1–10

---

#### Reframe Strength

Does it create a genuinely new way of seeing?

Score: 1–10

---

#### Memorability

Could somebody quote it later?

Score: 1–10

---

#### Portability

Can it be applied elsewhere?

Score: 1–10

---

## Scoring Guide

### 1–3

Weak.

Usually:

- Advice
- Observation
- Conclusion

Examples:

- Documentation is important.
- Startups should focus on users.

---

### 4–6

Interesting.

Contains some insight.

Limited explanatory power.

Examples:

- Startups focus too much on problem solving.

---

### 7–8

Strong.

Explains multiple observations.

Creates a useful reframe.

Examples:

- Utility gets adoption. Remarkability gets distribution.

---

### 9–10

Exceptional.

Creates a durable mental model.

Explains many observations.

Portable beyond the original domain.

Examples:

- Buildings have a memory problem.
- AI has a food supply problem.
- People don't share solutions. They share stories.

---

## Anti-Patterns

### Advice Disguised As Insight

Bad:

- Startups should delight users.

---

### Conclusion Disguised As Lens

Bad:

- Documentation is important.

---

### Observation Disguised As Lens

Bad:

- AI models train on AI-generated content.

---

### Clever But Doesn't Explain

Bad:

- Startup founders face a crisis of faith.

Why:

Memorable.

Explains little.

---

### Overfitted Lens

Bad:

A lens that explains only one example.

Strong lenses explain many examples.

## Golden Rule

The article is not the product.

The lens is the product.

The strongest lenses:

- Explain more than the original problem
- Resolve tension
- Create a category shift
- Become reusable mental shortcuts
- Generate new observations

The article merely earns the right to install the lens.
