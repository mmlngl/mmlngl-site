## Lens findings

### Strongest lens
The problem is not that founders care too much about solving problems.
The problem is that they mistake **utility** for **distribution**.

A product can solve a real problem and still fail because problem-solving earns usage, not conversation.

### Why this lens is stronger
- It explains why many competent products die anyway.
- It separates **being relevant** from **being spread**.
- It shifts the question from “does this work?” to “what makes this travel?”

### Distinctions this lens reveals
- **Need** gets you considered.
- **Preference** gets you chosen.
- **Social value** gets you talked about.

These are different games.

### Where the current draft is strongest
The most alive idea in the piece is:

> Being needed and being chosen are different games.

That feels like the core reframe.

### Where the draft can get sharper
- “Delight leads to success” is too narrow. Some products spread because they signal taste, status, competence, or insider knowledge — not just delight.
- “Nobody talks about toilets” is memorable, but it may over-index on low-consideration utilities. Some boring products still spread when recommending them makes the recommender look smart.
- “It’s not about the problem” is directionally right, but slightly overstated. Solving the problem is still table stakes; it’s just not the growth mechanism.

### Stronger articulation options
- Solving the problem makes a product useful. It does not make it spread.
- Problem-solution fit earns adoption. Social transmission earns growth.
- Founders are taught to optimise for utility when many markets reward remarkability.

### Candidate closing insight
The real question is not “what problem does this solve?”
It is:

**What does using or sharing this say about the person passing it on?**

## outline-from-lens

# Title
Utility gets adoption. Remarkability gets distribution.

## Lens
Utility gets adoption.
Remarkability gets distribution.

## Act 1 — Tension
- Open with the startup doctrine: founders are taught to ask, “What problem are we solving?”
- Show the contradiction: many products solve real problems and still fail.
- Use concrete examples:
  - Better to-do apps fail.
  - Better CRMs fail.
  - Better note-taking apps fail.
  - Better project management tools fail.
- Land the tension clearly:
  - If solving the problem were enough, these products should win.
  - But they don’t.
  - Something else is deciding outcomes.

## Act 2 — Investigation
- Separate usefulness from spread.
- Explore the idea that solving a problem gives someone a reason to use a product, but not a reason to mention it.
- Use the “nobody talks about toilets / filing cabinets / balanced spreadsheets” thread as evidence that utility often becomes invisible.
- Add the stronger nuance:
  - People don’t only share delight.
  - They share things that create signal: taste, status, competence, surprise, insider knowledge.
- Build the pattern:
  - Need creates consideration.
  - Preference creates choice.
  - Social value creates transmission.
- Explore why founders miss this:
  - Product culture overweights correctness.
  - Growth often depends on whether the product is story-worthy.
- Optional contrast examples:
  - Products people share because they make the user look clever.
  - Products that are slightly worse functionally but stronger socially.

## Act 3 — Lens
- Deliver the reframe only after the pattern feels obvious.
- Suggested turn:
  - Perhaps the real issue isn’t that founders fail to solve problems.
  - It’s that they confuse utility with distribution.
- Install the lens:
  - Utility gets adoption.
  - Remarkability gets distribution.
- Translate it:
  - Solving the problem is necessary.
  - But growth belongs to products people can retell.

## Closing
- End with the strategic question this lens creates:
  - What makes someone tell another person?
- Strong closing beat:
  - The real question is not just “what problem does this solve?”
  - It is “what does using or sharing this say about the person passing it on?”
- Final line option:
  - Solving the problem gets you used.
  - Giving people a story gets you spread.
