
\[Picking the Right Model for the job]

I'm concerned that Big-AI (Anthropic, Google, Openai) are forging ahead with new models and consumers are spoken to like they need to upgrade. Like newer models are new upgrades they get for free*. Like a new car or something.

ACTUALLY, it's just worse all round. Definitely worse for the environment. 
The tasks haven't changed: "write an email to my boss" – older models could do this just fine.

I'm not really sure who wins.

I watched a YOutUtbe video recently of a guy setting up openclaw. He recommended you use powerful models to help with tool selection. "TOOL selection"? Surely that's an easier task that i smaller model can handle.

There's a disconnect here between the frontier model providers and how consumers are using AI.


* Totally not free – you have limited usage within your subscription, the price of which is always increasing.

# Evaluation

## Existing Structure

### Everyone thinks

Newer AI models are upgrades, so users should want the latest model by default.

### But

For many common tasks, newer frontier models are not meaningfully better for the user.

### Because

The underlying tasks have not become harder, while providers are incentivised to push higher-cost capability.

### Which means

The consumer problem may be model routing and sufficiency, not access to ever-larger models.

## Lens

Current Lens:  
AI model progress is being marketed as consumer upgrade progress, even when the job does not require it.

Alternative Lenses:

1. **AI is selling horsepower to commuters.**
2. Consumer AI has a routing problem, not an intelligence problem.
3. Frontier labs are supply-pushing capability into demand that didn’t ask for it.
4. AI subscriptions hide metered scarcity behind upgrade language.
5. The industry mistakes benchmark progress for product progress.
6. Using frontier models for routine tasks is like using a crane to hang a picture.
7. Consumers do not need smarter models; they need better task-model matching.
8. AI providers are building Formula 1 cars for school runs.
9. The model market is optimising for prestige capacity, not everyday utility.
10. Frontier AI is overfitting to provider competition rather than user work.

## Findings

### Observations

- Providers frame new models as default upgrades.
- Many consumer tasks are unchanged and low complexity.
- Usage is constrained despite “subscription” framing.
- People recommend powerful models for surprisingly lightweight tasks.
- There is a mismatch between frontier competition and ordinary usage.

### Pattern

Capability is being pushed downstream as if all demand benefits from maximum intelligence.

### Tension

People expect:  
Better models automatically mean better consumer value.

Yet:  
Most users are doing repetitive, low-stakes, low-complexity work where sufficiency matters more than frontier capability.

### Why The Lens Works

“Consumers have a routing problem, not an intelligence problem” explains:
- why old models still feel fine for many tasks
- why tool selection likely doesn’t need frontier reasoning
- why subscriptions feel fake-scarce
- why cost and environmental concerns keep surfacing
- why vendors keep shipping things users didn’t explicitly need

### Why The Lens Fails

- Some tasks really do benefit from stronger models.
- The piece currently assumes, more than proves, environmental and economic waste.
- It needs more concrete task taxonomy: what actually needs frontier capability vs not.

## Expansion Test

If this lens is true, what else does it explain?

1. Why chat apps now expose many model choices.
2. Why “auto” model selection is becoming a product feature.
3. Why users resent limits on “premium” usage.
4. Why small local models remain attractive.
5. Why benchmark wins don’t always translate to user delight.
6. Why enterprise buyers care about cost-per-task more than flagship status.
7. Why agents need orchestration layers.
8. Why latency often matters more than maximum capability.
9. Why many AI products converge on the same few demos.
10. Why the next UX battleground is model selection invisibility.

## Risks / Unknowns

- Could sound like “new models are useless,” which is too broad.
- Needs evidence that smaller models are good enough for tool selection.
- Needs sharper distinction between consumer, prosumer, and enterprise use.

## Recommendation

Recommended Lens:  
**Consumers don’t have an intelligence problem. They have a routing problem.**

Reason:  
It creates the strongest category shift, explains the most observations, and points to a real product implication: the winner may not be the company with the smartest model, but the one that best matches task to model invisibly.

## Stronger Reframe

Everyone thinks:  
AI gets better when the default model gets smarter.

But:  
For most users, smarter defaults are often the wrong product.

Because:  
The real bottleneck is not insufficient intelligence, but poor routing between tasks and the amount of intelligence they actually require.

Which means:  
The next important AI product is not the biggest model, but the system that quietly uses the smallest sufficient one.

# Big AI Is Selling F1 Cars for a Trip to Tesco

This week, big AI released another "frontier" model. 
Can you feel the hype?

Every AI company wants me to believe I need their newest and best model.
"Powerful". "Smarter". "More capable".

Yet, the tasks I ask it to do haven't changed:
Fix a bug.
Summarise a doc.
Answer a question.

Bugs haven't gotten more complex.
Docs haven't gotten longer.
My questions haven't gotten deeper.

In 2020, GPT-3 could write an email to my boss.
In 2023, GPT-4 could write an email to my boss.
In 2024, Gemini could write an email to my boss.
In 2025, Claude could write an email to my boss.

Whatever frontier launched this week, whatever model launches next month, I'll can use it to bug my boss.

Yet, every launch is sold to me like my life is about to change.

It feels like being pushed to upgrade without a change in usefulness.

*

In 2025, [METR showed that each Frontier model is better at completing complex tasks](https://metr.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks/). They assumed more complex tasks take longer and compared completion % to task duration.
Result showed that new models are more successful at longer tasks.

But what if the task duration is the same?
What if the complexity doesn't change?
I'm not asking it to refactor a codebase.
I'm asking it to summarise a document.

Openclaw became popular over the last year.
It's an ecosystem and community focused on give AI superpowers to your life

It's popularity caused an explosion of YT videos describing how to set it up.
There's a step in that process when you pick the AI brain you want it to use.

In one video I watched, the guy suggested you set up multiple models. One for small, simple tasks. One more powerful for complex tasks.

He went on: "Use the powerful model for tool selection".

"Tool selection"? That's one of the easier tasks.
The distinction between "check my calendar" and "send an email" is clear.

*

It made me wonder whether we’re confusing capability with usefulness.

We're not being lied to. Newer models _are_ more intelligent. 

Perhaps we don't have an intelligence problem.
We have a model-picking problem.

There's no challenge in finding the smartest model.
It's knowing much intelligence the task actually requires.

The AI industry is obsessed with building faster F1 cars.
Most of us are driving to Tesco.

The next true breakthrough might not be a smarter model.
It might be a system that quietly picks the smallest sufficient one.
